## The method, in one sentence

A pitch is an argument about a future business, made credible by the
business that already exists — and the deck is that argument's skeleton
written as page titles, one point per page, with the data as the body that
proves each point.

## The ten working rules

1. **The vertebrae come first.** Write the narrative as 10–15 bullet
   points, each one point, each provable; under each, list the data that
   proves it. One bullet becomes one page: the bullet is the title, the
   data is the body. A page trying to make two points is two pages, and a
   deck is 10–15 pages plus appendix. (Tam's "fundraising vertebrae" —
   the same discipline as red line 4, bound harder: the concatenated
   titles ARE the investment argument.)
2. **The title is the claim, with its number in it.** The Series A
   program deck titles every content page as a full finding sentence —
   "We help 36 companies raise their As each quarter, growing at 78% q/q"
   — and the body exists to prove the sentence. A topic label ("Traction")
   wastes the strongest line on the page.
3. **An investor pitch is not a customer pitch.** The reader has not met
   the problem, does not know the jargon, and is deciding whether to
   spend an hour, not whether to buy. Three things, in order: the problem
   and its impact on the paying customer, how the product changes that
   customer's experience, and why that is a good business.
4. **Evidence before vision.** Open with what is already built and
   measured; the vision page spends credibility the traction pages
   earned. Hypotheticals mixed into the present read as compensation and
   get discounted to zero. The vision page then dreams on purpose — "how
   this becomes a $10B company" — and is licensed to, because everything
   before it was real.
5. **Traction states trends, not points.** A monthly or quarterly line
   beats an annual sum; four to six months of trend is the floor of
   believability (a floor, not a target); time-to-milestone is the speed
   evidence. Cumulative-only charts read as hiding the monthly numbers;
   double-axis charts burn minutes clarifying which line owns which axis.
   Every number defines what it measures — WAU or DAU, gross or net.
6. **A few hero facts beat reams of numbers.** Identify the headline
   numbers that prove the key points — the ones an investor repeats to
   their partners — and guide the reader to them. Too many numbers is
   information overload; too few reads as hiding; a confusingly computed
   number reads as incompetence or dishonesty, which is worse than a
   weak one.
7. **Problem and solution mirror each other.** The problem page shows how
   the world works today, concretely, with numbers, from the paying
   customer's side — scoped to the part this product actually solves. The
   solution page is the side-by-side: the same steps with the product,
   quantified ("C% more, D× faster"). The seed deck's problem and
   solution pages carry the same numbered structure, so the mirror is
   visible. Screenshots and feature lists do neither; a bulleted list of
   the customer's steps does both.
8. **Market is a bottoms-up equation, drawn.** Prospective customers ×
   value of each, from the business's own current numbers — the Series A
   deck draws it as a labeled arithmetic band (1,000 × $1B × 0.1% = $1B,
   each factor captioned). Top-down report numbers are the named misuse;
   a category creator adds analogy companies instead.
9. **The ask is the climax, never omitted.** Amount, what it buys at a
   high level, and where that lands the company in 18–24 months, in
   traction terms — the deck ends by showing money is the binding
   constraint, not competence or product-market fit. The one-liner obeys
   the same discipline the ask does: concrete, layperson terms, specific
   ("we build and operate robot greenhouses", never "reinventing
   agriculture").
10. **Legible, simple, obvious — the glance test.** Large bold type, high
    contrast, text top-of-slide; one idea per page; the conclusion written
    on the chart as its caption, so the reader who looks up from email
    still gets it. Show a slide to a stranger: if they cannot say the
    point immediately, the slide loses. Diagrams are mazes for ideas
    (Hick's law) — the one licensed exception is deliberate overwhelm,
    when the point IS the complexity. Design aids comprehension over
    beauty; investors invest in teams, not slides.

## What this changes for a LUMI page

The palette, the waterline, the figure grammar and the record band stay
LUMI's (brand.md is identity, not up for imitation) — a LUMI BP is a LUMI
deck telling YC's argument shape, not a grey Google-Slides imitation. What
the study adds is the ARGUMENT discipline: vertebrae before any page, the
title as the claim with its number, evidence strictly before vision, the
mirrored problem/solution pair, the bottoms-up market band, the ask as the
climax. Where YC's bare-bones design advice and LUMI's design language
disagree (YC tolerates chartless pages of grey bullets; LUMI's D16/D29
do not), LUMI's rules win — the YC template is a floor for clarity, never
a ceiling on composition. And YC's own template deck stamps "all numbers
are for illustration purposes" on its title page: a scaffold deck built
ahead of real data labels itself the same way, which is red line 1 doing
its ordinary work.
